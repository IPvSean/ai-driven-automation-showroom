= Section 1.2 - Smart Alerts and Dual-Path Remediation

:navtitle: Smart Alerts and Dual-Path Remediation

*Estimated time: 30-40 minutes*

== Objective

In this section you will connect Instana to Ansible Automation Platform and exercise both remediation paths. You will create a webhook alert channel, an Application Perspective, and a Smart Alert, then break the Flask application and watch Event-Driven Ansible restore it automatically. Finally you will break it again with automation disarmed, and see which remediation Instana recommends for the incident and why.

By the end of this module you will have a working closed loop: Instana detects the outage, automation repairs it, and Instana confirms the recovery.

== Learning Objectives

After completing this module, you will be able to:

* Identify the Ansible job templates provisioned for your environment and explain how they are isolated per student
* Create a generic webhook alert channel that targets your Event-Driven Ansible listener
* Create an Application Perspective and a service-level Smart Alert scoped to it
* Trigger and observe fully automated remediation through Event-Driven Ansible
* Read the NLP-ranked remediation recommendations Instana surfaces on a live incident
* Explain when each path is the right choice

== Prerequisites

Before starting, confirm:

* You can log in to the Instana UI with the credentials from Section 1.1
* You can log in to link:{controller_url}[Ansible Automation Platform^] as `{controller_username}`
* Your service `lab-webapp-{guid}` appears in the Instana Services tab and is receiving calls

[IMPORTANT]
====
Instana is shared with every other student in this workshop. Name the alert channel, Application Perspective, and Smart Alert you are about to create with your own `{guid}`, exactly as shown. Do not modify or delete objects belonging to another `{guid}`.
====

== Step 1: Review Your Ansible Job Templates

The lab provisioned six job templates for you in a dedicated Ansible Automation Platform organization. Look at them before wiring anything up, because their names and descriptions are what Instana matches against later.

. Open link:{controller_url}[Ansible Automation Platform^]
. Navigate to **Automation Execution** -> **Templates**
. Search for `{guid}`

You will see six templates, all in the **Instana AIOps** organization:

[cols="3,2", options="header"]
|===
| Template
| Purpose

| `INSTANA \| {guid} \| Restore Web App Service - Fix anomalously low calls`
| Starts `lab-webapp` and verifies health. This is the remediation.

| `INSTANA \| {guid} \| Break Web App Service - Simulate low calls outage`
| Stops `lab-webapp` to simulate the outage.

| `INSTANA \| {guid} \| Check Web App Service Health - Diagnose low calls incident`
| Reports the systemd service status.

| `INSTANA \| {guid} \| Collect Web App Diagnostics - Investigate low call rate`
| Gathers host facts for triage.

| `INSTANA \| {guid} \| AI Route Web App Incident - Analyze anomalously low calls`
| Entry point for AI-driven routing.

| `INSTANA \| {guid} \| Notify Mattermost - Web App Low Calls Alert`
| Sends a notification to Mattermost.
|===

[NOTE]
====
Every template name begins with `INSTANA | {guid} |`. That prefix is what keeps a shared Instana instance safe: your alert can only ever launch templates carrying your own `{guid}`, and you can find your own work in a catalog containing every student's templates.

Notice also that the descriptions repeat phrases such as "anomalously low calls", "service is down", and "unreachable". That wording is deliberate, and Exercise 2 explains why.
====

== Step 2: Confirm the Event-Driven Ansible Listener Is Running

Path 1 depends on a rulebook activation that is already running for you.

. In Ansible Automation Platform, navigate to **Automation Decisions** -> **Rulebook Activations**
. Confirm that **Instana AIOps** shows status **Running**

image::instana_eda_activation_running.png[Instana AIOps rulebook activation running, link="_images/instana_eda_activation_running.png", window=blank]

This activation runs the `instana.yml` rulebook, which listens for webhooks on port 5000 and acts only on events where `issue.state` is `OPEN`.

Your webhook URL is:

[source, text]
----
{eda_webhook_url}
----

Copy it. You need it in the next step.

[NOTE]
====
The listener runs inside Ansible Automation Platform as a rulebook activation, not as a service you manage on the bastion. To inspect what it has received, open the **Instana AIOps** activation and use its **History** tab rather than looking for logs over SSH.
====

== Step 3: Create a Generic Webhook Alert Channel in Instana

An alert channel tells Instana where to send a notification when an alert fires. Create it now, because you will attach it to the Smart Alert in the next step.

. In the Instana UI, click **Settings** in the left navigation menu
. Navigate to **Alerting** -> **Alert Channels**

image::instana_settings_alerting.png[Instana Alerting Settings, link="_images/instana_settings_alerting.png", window=blank]

[start=3]
. Click **Add Alert Channel**
. Complete the form:

[cols="2,3", options="header"]
|===
| Field
| Value

| Alert Channel Type
| Generic Webhook

| Name
| `AIOps Automated Remediation - {guid}`

| Webhook URL
| `{eda_webhook_url}`
|===

image::instana_webhook_channel_config.png[Webhook Channel Configuration, link="_images/instana_webhook_channel_config.png", window=blank]

[start=5]
. Leave the remaining fields at their defaults and click **Create**

[TIP]
====
If the channel configuration offers a **Send Test Alert** button, use it to prove connectivity. The activation **History** in Ansible Automation Platform will show the event arriving.

No job will launch, and that is expected. The test payload is a `change` event with no `state` field, so it cannot satisfy the rulebook condition `issue.state == "OPEN"`. Only a genuine firing alert launches remediation.
====

== Step 4: Create the Application Perspective and Smart Alert

Instana does not let you attach a Smart Alert directly to a service. Smart Alerts are created in the context of an **Application Perspective**, so this is a two stage process:

. Create an Application Perspective containing your service
. Create a Smart Alert inside that Application Perspective

=== Stage 1: Create your Application Perspective

An Application Perspective is a scoped view of one or more services. Yours will contain a single service, `lab-webapp-{guid}`.

. In the Instana UI, click **Applications** in the left navigation menu
. Click **Add Application Perspective**
. Name it:
+
[source, text]
----
{guid} Web App
----
+
. For the scope, select your service: `lab-webapp-{guid}`
. Save the Application Perspective

image::instana_application_perspective.png[Application Perspective, link="_images/instana_application_perspective.png", window=blank]

[IMPORTANT]
====
Select only `lab-webapp-{guid}`. Every student's Flask service is visible in this shared instance, and an Application Perspective scoped too broadly will alert on other students' services as well as your own.
====

Allow a short time for the Application Perspective to populate with data before continuing.

=== Stage 2: Create the Smart Alert

. Open your `{guid} Web App` Application Perspective
. Go to its **Smart Alerts** tab
. Click to create a new Smart Alert
. Switch the alert to **Advanced mode**
. Select **Throughput** as the alert type, then choose the **Calls** metric
. Configure the condition:

[cols="2,3", options="header"]
|===
| Field
| Value

| Alert Name
| `{guid} Service Down - Calls Drop`

| Mode
| Advanced

| Alert type
| Throughput

| Metric
| Calls

| Condition
| `< 1`, severity Critical

| Evaluation granularity
| 1 minute

| Alert Channel
| `AIOps Automated Remediation - {guid}`
|===

image::instana_smart_alert_config.png[Smart Alert Configuration, link="_images/instana_smart_alert_config.png", window=blank]

[start=7]
. Attach the alert channel `AIOps Automated Remediation - {guid}` that you created in Step 3
. Review the configuration and save the alert

[IMPORTANT]
====
The alert channel must be attached to the Smart Alert itself. The channel existing on its own does nothing. If you skip this, the alert will fire in the Instana UI but no webhook will reach Event-Driven Ansible, and Path 1 will appear to do nothing at all.
====

[NOTE]
====
Advanced mode is required because the default Smart Alert mode uses an automatic baseline. You want a deterministic threshold here: fire when calls fall below one per minute. That makes the exercise reproducible within a workshop slot.

The saved alert may be displayed under a generic default title. That label is cosmetic. What matters is the configuration you entered. When it fires, the payload reports `metricName: calls` and the text "Number of calls is anomalously low".
====

== Step 5: Confirm Your Actions Reached the Instana Action Catalog

Instana polls Ansible Automation Platform with a dedicated read and execute account and imports the job templates that account can see. Those imported templates become **actions**.

. In the Instana UI, open the **Action Catalog**
. Search for `{guid}`

image::instana_action_catalog_list.png[Action Catalog List, link="_images/instana_action_catalog_list.png", window=blank]

You should find your six actions, each of type **ANSIBLE**.

[NOTE]
====
Import is performed by the Instana backend polling Ansible Automation Platform on a schedule of roughly five minutes. If your actions are not listed yet, continue with the module and check again shortly.

The import is scoped by the credentials Instana uses, which only have access to the **Instana AIOps** organization. Instana cannot see or execute templates belonging to the rest of the lab.
====

== Exercise 1: Path 1 - Automated Remediation

Break the service and let Event-Driven Ansible repair it without you.

=== Break the service

. In Ansible Automation Platform, navigate to **Automation Execution** -> **Templates**
. Search for `{guid}` and open `INSTANA | {guid} | Break Web App Service - Simulate low calls outage`
. Click **Launch**
. The template prompts for a **Limit**. Enter:
+
[source, text]
----
{rhel_node2}
----
+
. Click **Launch** to confirm

image::aap_break_template.png[Break Template, link="_images/aap_break_template.png", window=blank]

[IMPORTANT]
====
The limit is required. These templates run against the shared lab inventory, and the limit is what confines the play to your own application host. Launching without it targets more hosts than intended.
====

The job stops `lab-webapp.service` on `{rhel_node2}`. The Flask application stops answering, and the traffic generator on the bastion starts failing its health checks.

=== Watch the calls drop

. Switch to Instana, open **Services** -> `lab-webapp-{guid}`
. Watch the **Calls** metric fall to zero within a few seconds

image::instana_calls_drop_graph.png[Calls Drop to Zero, link="_images/instana_calls_drop_graph.png", window=blank]

=== Wait for the Smart Alert to open

Allow roughly one to two minutes. The Smart Alert uses a one minute evaluation granularity, so it waits for a full minute below threshold before firing. This is what prevents a brief hiccup from triggering automation.

When it fires, the incident appears in Instana with state **OPEN** and the text "Number of calls is anomalously low".

image::instana_alert_open.png[Alert OPEN, link="_images/instana_alert_open.png", window=blank]

=== Confirm Event-Driven Ansible received the webhook

. In Ansible Automation Platform, navigate to **Automation Decisions** -> **Rulebook Activations**
. Open **Instana AIOps**
. Open the **History** tab to see the received event and the rule that matched

The rulebook extracts `{guid}` from the `entityLabel` field of the payload and launches the matching restore template.

=== Watch the restore job run

. Navigate to **Automation Execution** -> **Jobs**
. A job named `INSTANA | {guid} | Restore Web App Service - Fix anomalously low calls` should be running, launched by automation rather than by you

image::aap_restore_job_auto_triggered.png[Auto-Triggered Restore Job, link="_images/aap_restore_job_auto_triggered.png", window=blank]

[start=3]
. Open the job and wait for it to finish

The job starts `lab-webapp.service` on `{rhel_node2}` and verifies the health endpoint responds.

=== Confirm recovery in Instana

. Return to Instana, **Services** -> `lab-webapp-{guid}`
. The **Calls** metric climbs back to its normal level as the traffic generator succeeds again
. After a minute or two above threshold, the incident closes automatically and the state becomes **CLOSED**

image::instana_calls_restored.png[Calls Restored, link="_images/instana_calls_restored.png", window=blank]

The rulebook also matches `CLOSED` events and logs the recovery, which you can see in the activation history.

You have completed Path 1. The sequence was:

. Service stops
. Instana detects the drop, alert opens after about one minute
. Webhook reaches the rulebook activation
. The activation launches your restore template
. The job restarts the service in about thirty seconds
. Instana observes recovery and closes the incident

No human took any action after the service failed.

== Exercise 2: Path 2 - Operator-Driven Remediation from the Action Catalog

Now look at the same outage from the operator's side: what Instana recommends, and why.

=== Disarm Path 1 first

Path 1 is still armed. If you break the service now, Event-Driven Ansible will repair it before you have a chance to act.

. In Ansible Automation Platform, navigate to **Automation Decisions** -> **Rulebook Activations**
. Select **Instana AIOps**
. **Disable** the activation and wait for it to stop

[IMPORTANT]
====
Re-enable the **Instana AIOps** activation when you finish this exercise. Later sections assume it is running, and leaving it disabled will make the Section 1 behaviour impossible to reproduce.
====

=== Break the service again

Repeat the break step from Exercise 1: launch `INSTANA | {guid} | Break Web App Service - Simulate low calls outage` with the limit `{rhel_node2}`.

Wait for the Smart Alert to open in Instana as before. This time nothing repairs it, because the webhook has nowhere to go.

=== Read the recommended action

. In Instana, open the incident for `lab-webapp-{guid}`
. Review the actions Instana recommends for this incident

image::instana_incident_recommendations.png[Recommended actions on the incident, link="_images/instana_incident_recommendations.png", window=blank]

Instana compares the incident text, "Number of calls is anomalously low", against the names and descriptions of the actions in its catalog, and ranks them by similarity. Your restore action is described as handling the case "when number of calls is anomalously low or zero ... service is down, unreachable, or call rate has dropped significantly", so it scores as a relevant recommendation.

[NOTE]
====
This matching is natural language processing built into Instana. It runs against the imported action metadata, with no external large language model involved, which is why it works in a disconnected on-premise deployment.

The quality of the recommendation depends entirely on how the Ansible job templates are described. The descriptions in this lab were written to contain the vocabulary that appears in the alert. Vague template descriptions produce weak recommendations, which is a practical argument for treating template metadata as part of your automation interface.
====

=== Open the recommended action

. Select `INSTANA | {guid} | Restore Web App Service - Fix anomalously low calls`
. Review its details: the action type is **ANSIBLE**, and it maps to the job template you inspected in Step 1

image::instana_action_restore_details.png[Action Details, link="_images/instana_action_restore_details.png", window=blank]

This is the same job template that Event-Driven Ansible launched for you in Exercise 1. Instana is not holding a copy of the automation. It holds a reference to your job template and calls the Ansible Automation Platform API to run it.

[IMPORTANT]
====
**You cannot execute the action from Instana in this workshop.** The shared Instana instance used here is configured in a way that does not permit running actions from the UI. This is a limitation of the lab environment, not of the integration.

In a production Instana deployment, this is exactly where the operator clicks **Run** and the remediation executes, with the run attributed to them. Everything up to that click is identical to what you are looking at now.
====

=== Restore the service

Since you cannot run the action from Instana here, restore the service from Ansible Automation Platform instead. This runs the same job template that the Instana action points at.

. In Ansible Automation Platform, navigate to **Automation Execution** -> **Templates**
. Open `INSTANA | {guid} | Restore Web App Service - Fix anomalously low calls`
. Click **Launch**, enter the limit `{rhel_node2}`, and confirm
. Wait for the job to complete, then return to Instana and confirm calls resume and the incident closes

=== Re-enable Path 1

. In Ansible Automation Platform, navigate to **Automation Decisions** -> **Rulebook Activations**
. **Enable** the **Instana AIOps** activation
. Confirm it returns to **Running**

== Comparing the Two Paths

You saw the same remediation reached two different ways: once triggered by a rule, once selected by a person.

[cols="1,2,2", options="header"]
|===
| Aspect
| Path 1: Event-Driven Ansible
| Path 2: Action Catalog

| Trigger
| Webhook from the Smart Alert
| Operator selects an action in Instana

| Decision made by
| A rule written in advance
| A person, at incident time

| Time to remediation
| About two minutes, unattended
| As fast as someone responds

| Attribution
| The rulebook activation
| The operator who ran the action

| Choice of remediation
| Fixed by the rule
| Chosen from ranked recommendations

| Best suited to
| Known failure modes with a safe, single fix
| Ambiguous incidents, or changes requiring judgement

| In this workshop
| Fully functional
| Recommendations are visible; execution is disabled on the shared instance
|===

Use Path 1 where the failure mode is understood and the fix is safe to apply unattended. Use Path 2 where the correct response depends on context a rule cannot capture, or where your change process requires a person to decide.

Most organisations run both. The value of the Action Catalog is that the operator making the judgement call is handed a short, ranked list of validated automation instead of a blank terminal.

== Summary

You have:

* Reviewed the per-student job templates and seen how the `{guid}` prefix isolates each environment
* Created a generic webhook alert channel pointing at your rulebook activation
* Created an Application Perspective and a Smart Alert scoped to your own service
* Watched Event-Driven Ansible detect and repair an outage with no human involvement
* Read the remediation Instana recommended against a live incident, and seen that it points at your own job template
* Compared the two paths on speed, attribution, and flexibility

The wider point: observability is not only about seeing problems. Once an observability platform can both describe an incident and reach your automation, detection and repair become one loop rather than two disconnected activities.

In the sections that follow you will extend this pattern with AI-driven route selection, approval gates, and closed-loop verification.
